How I check the work

How I keep important numbers tied to their sources.

A handful of habits that make it easier to catch weak data, late information, lucky results, and overconfident conclusions before they turn into decisions.

It isn't a promise that every project is perfect. It's how I make sure every important number can be traced back to where it came from.

See the six questions How I work →
The six questions

Every serious project answers the same six questions.

The details change. The questions stay deliberately boring.

01 Question. What decision is this supposed to improve?
02 Rule. What result would count as useful, weak, or disqualifying?
03 Inputs. Is the data complete, current, and right for the question?
04 Run. Could someone repeat it from the same data and written steps?
05 Check. Did a test, a comparison, or a tough example try to break the result?
06 Decision. What's allowed to happen next, and who makes that call?
The checks, and what they caught

Every check here has changed a real answer at least once.

Each one exists because numbers have gone wrong this way before: late data, a moved goalpost, a convenient comparison.

What an evidence record is

An evidence record isn't a wall of technical output.

It's the shortest useful answer to: “Where did this come from, and what would make me doubt it?” Depending on the project, that might be a data file, a test result, a dated log, or a record of a decision.

A rule for any reader If a page gives you a strong conclusion but can't tell you what was measured, when, and what would change the conclusion, treat it as a pitch, not proof.
What this doesn't promise

Checks reduce avoidable mistakes. They don't make uncertainty disappear.

None of this promises perfect data, perfect models, business success, or a right answer every time.

It does promise a better question, a visible rule and a traceable source, a real chance to catch a result that's wrong, and an honest note about what's still unknown.
