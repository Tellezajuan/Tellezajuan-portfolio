Marketing measurement · plan written in advance · public dataset

Late-arriving conversions made reported cost per customer look about 4× too low.

A study using public advertising data to ask what the report knew when the budget decision was due.

I tested whether conversions recorded later could make an old campaign report look better than the information available when the decision was made. I wrote the test plan before looking at the result.

The finding Campaigns with late-arriving credit looked about 4× cheaper than they were. Among the 25 campaigns with the most late-arriving conversions, the middle result was 297% higher after correction. A separate check on data kept out of the first analysis improved the public-data comparison by 50.8%. The problem

A conversion recorded today may belong to last week's budget decision.

Nothing necessarily broke in tracking. A report can receive a conversion after the budget decision and file that credit into the older campaign story. That timing gap is where the problem hides: the campaign looks stronger than the evidence available when the decision was made.

This study measures that gap directly on a dataset anyone can download.

The human work is choosing the cutoff, writing the rule down before seeing the result, and deciding what the evidence allows next.

How the correction works

Score the old report, rebuild the decision-time view, then choose what deserves another test.

The finding

The leak is real, it is large, and it is not random noise.

CHECK 1 · PLANNED IN ADVANCE Scrambling the timing produces almost nothing. As a false-alarm check, I scrambled the timing and re-ran the test 20 times. The scrambled runs produced a middle result of 0 candidates and never more than 1. The real timing produced 33.
CHECK 2 · CONFIRMED AT LARGER SCALE 999 more timing shuffles tell the same story. Across 999 scrambled runs, the middle result was 0 and the highest was 4. None matched or exceeded the real result; the statistical check reports p = 0.001.
What changes

A correction matters only if it changes the next budget decision.

The real test of a correction is whether it changes what you would have done. On public data kept out of the first analysis, the first rule selects 274 campaigns at a cost per customer of 0.01782. The corrected rule selects a different, smaller set: 99 campaigns at 0.00877, a 50.8% improvement in this public-data comparison, not proof of live campaign lift.

One anonymous campaign reports a cost per customer of 0.00828 before the correction and 0.05076 after: a 6.1× difference caused by 431 late-arriving credits. These are the public dataset's own units, not dollars. The source identifier stays in the evidence record so the example can be checked.

Two implementation bugs were caught before the result was treated as evidence. One check was first compared against the wrong baseline, and a second miscounted which campaigns belonged in the test group. Both were fixed, everything downstream was re-run, and the original plan stayed intact.

A question for your own reports

Does this happen in your account?

Probably, and it is not anyone's fault. Many ad platforms backfill conversions into the day of the click, not the day the conversion occurred. That is the right choice for understanding history and the wrong one for judging a decision you already made.

01 Pull the same report twice. Once today, once from a snapshot 30 days ago. If the older numbers have changed, you have the problem.
02 Check your click-to-conversion lag. Anything longer than your reporting cycle is the size of your blind spot.
03 Replay your last budget decision. Ask what it would have looked like using only the data that existed the morning you made it.

What it took

The result only became useful after two uncomfortable checks.

Time and scale
The public dataset made the question repeatable by someone else, but it also meant every denominator had to stay visible.
What broke
Late credit changed the apparent answer, and the first weekly prediction written before analysis did not fully match the result.
Next time
I would state every denominator and caveat in the first layer, before asking a reader to trust the headline.
Why it belongs
The claim is not just the 4× gap; it is a measured answer that survived timing, credit-rule, false-alarm, and week-by-week checks.
