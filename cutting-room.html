YouTube video workflow · production operations · release checks

I turned a noisy clip collection into a repeatable video pipeline.

It finds clips, narrows the review set, builds drafts, checks the output, and leaves release approval to me.

Over a 32-day production window, 33,365 clips entered the search pool and 150 reached me for review. A separate production record counts 85 published outputs; it is not the next step in that same funnel. The system keeps moving when one source fails and stops a video when its final checks do not pass.

Production window: July 22–August 23, 2026.

Why I built it

Find more. Watch less.

A gaming channel built from raw clips turns into a chore fast: thousands of files and no reliable way to tell which five minutes are worth watching back. I wanted a system that does the tedious narrowing automatically and leaves the judgment call, is this moment actually good, to me.

The two tiers are intentional: broad category search finds channels I would never know to look for, while the 117-creator watchlist keeps channels I trust visible. The design goal is not to remove taste. It is to give taste a much better starting point.

How it works

Discover → Collect → Narrow → Review → Approve.

What it actually took to build

Seven systems make the five-stage story work.

The five-step overview keeps the story easy to follow; this is the complete operating chain underneath it.

01 Finding the material. Three platforms, 16,479 channels surfaced by broad search, plus 117 creators I deliberately keep on the watchlist.
02 Cutting the noise. A ranking rule weighs how quickly views are growing, how often a channel produces clips, the creator, language, and dozens of title keywords.
03 Watching the video itself. The system finds scene changes and action, then picks the moment. It also finds the creator’s camera box so the vertical crop keeps the reaction in frame.
04 Making it publishable. The system stitches the video layers together, adds the frame and opening and closing cards, and balances the sound automatically.
05 Naming it. Titles, descriptions, tags, and ranked thumbnail candidates, generated automatically.
06 Owner review. The shortlist lands in a spreadsheet. Approve, reject, pick a thumbnail.
07 Shipping it. Upload to YouTube and TikTok, scheduled to go out on its own once I have approved it.
Work removed

Repetitive narrowing, not judgment.

The pipeline handles discovery, assembly, and routine checks. I still review the shortlist, approve clips, and choose the thumbnail.

Failure boundary

One bad source cannot stop the queue.

Source errors are logged and isolated. A video-length check protects the approved lineup, and the final check has kept working through 3,752 logged errors.

Build conditions

Three constraints shaped the system.

A limited daily work window, a noisy source pool, and a human approval gate determined what needed automation and what needed me.

The audience result

The channel is young, and it started from a standing start.

The pipeline above is the production discipline. This is what the channel did with it in its first 28 days live. Growth this fast is normal for a brand-new channel before the curve settles; it is not a claim that this rate holds.

47.4K views, first 28 days
304.7 watch-time hours, first 28 days
1,693 average views per day
+20 subscribers, first month

Self-reported from YouTube Studio for the first 28 days the channel was live. Kept separate from the production evidence on purpose: the production record shows that the workflow is real and selective; this shows what the audience did afterward.

The 33,284 remainder means clips not marked used in an output record (33,365 minus 81); it includes clips still waiting for review.

Operational effort: the system logged 3,752 source or processing errors during the snapshot. It isolates one failing source rather than treating every error as a failed run.
