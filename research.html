Research lab · simulation only

I test trading and sports-betting ideas without risking a dollar.

Two small labs share one rule. One studies stock options, contracts that let someone buy or sell a stock at a set price later. The other studies sports betting markets. Every idea is written down with what would prove it wrong, tested on past data, and kept away from real money.

446 trading ideas and 814 sports questions so far. Most fail, and that is the point. By my rough estimate the two labs need about half an hour of my attention a day.

The problem

Finding something interesting is easy. Knowing whether it’s real is the job.

A test on past data can draw a beautiful line that means nothing, especially when nobody wrote the question down first. That goes for a stock chart and for a sports hunch, like a team scoring more goals after a long midweek match.

Both labs make challenging the result part of the routine: a limited list of questions, fair comparisons, simulated money only, and a record of everything that got rejected.

In plain terms

Every idea has to survive the same loop.

01Write the question. Say exactly what the idea should explain.
02Decide what would prove it wrong. Set the comparison and failure rule before any data is checked.
03Replay history. Test against past market or game data and keep the records.
04Challenge the result. Compare it with a simpler explanation, simulated money only.
05Record what happened. Pass, fail, no real pattern, or too little data, all dated.
06Check before the next run. See what changed, and keep real money out.

Lab two · sports betting markets

Every sports idea gets a written “prove me wrong” rule before it’s tested.

It’s tempting to notice a pattern and keep adjusting the test until the pattern survives. I flip that: before any test runs, I write down the condition that would end the idea. Researchers call that a falsifier.

The automation refreshes the data; I write the rules, read the results, and decide whether an idea moves on. The answer is allowed to be boring.

System checks

The research is only as trustworthy as the system running it.

The steps that produce a conclusion get checked on a schedule, not only when a result looks surprising.

Tests 952 automated tests pass in the trading lab. They exercise 48.8% of the code: a safety net, not full coverage.
Problems caught 11 problems logged; the typical one was noticed within about a day. Each stays in the log with what broke and when it was caught.
One example A broken run was kept out of the results. A health check stopped an incomplete run before any conclusion was recorded.
Automation 65 scheduled tasks keep both labs running. I read results instead of watching every step; what the system may do stays the same.
What it took

The interesting part is how often the answer was no.

How long
Built mostly between early July and mid-September 2026, going by the project’s file dates. Both labs keep running on their own schedules.
What broke
An incomplete trading run, repeated after the fix and kept in the log. On the sports side, two ideas that looked promising were later disproved by my own follow-up checks.
Do differently
Show the untested gaps sooner, and show why each idea stopped at the check that stopped it, so nobody has to guess.

How all 814 sports questions reconcile

The first 517 break down into 393 process failures, 118 data failures, 4 grouping problems, and 2 suspicious results. The other 297 reached later checks: 126 failed, 71 did not have enough evidence, 45 failed a fair-comparison check, 43 were replaced by a better version of the question, 9 had no clear answer, and 3 looked promising. Together those groups account for all 814 questions.
