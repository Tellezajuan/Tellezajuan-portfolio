Marketing Measurement → Technical appendix

How I tested whether campaign reports counted late-arriving conversions.

The short answer: I tried to make the finding disappear before I trusted it.

I scrambled the timing, changed who received credit, checked for false winners, and repeated the comparison week by week. The finding did not depend on one convenient chart.

The timing problem

Most conversion credit arrived after the decision.

The campaign report kept changing after the budget choice had already been made. That is the gap the rest of the checks investigate.

If you want the reasoning

Open the question you want to inspect.

Did changing who gets credit change the answer?

Yes—45 campaign decisions changed. On the same corrected data, 100 passed when the final click received all credit and 81 passed when credit was shared across several touches.

Could testing many campaigns create false winners?

The public data contains 675 campaigns. 59 did not have enough activity for this comparison, leaving 616. The first screen found 530 apparent winners; 480 remained after the stricter false-winner check.

For technical readers, the recorded cutoff was q = 0.1 and p < 0.002.

Did the direction hold week by week?

Of 652 campaigns with enough weekly activity, 473 stayed at or below the comparison line, 93 stayed above it, and 86 moved between the two.

The first week agreed with the full-data answer 88.4% of the time. The prediction written before analysis did not fully match what happened, so the mismatch remains in the record.

What did the campaigns that survived have in common?

They had 2.62× the ad views, 1.27× the click rate, and 0.16× the cost per customer of campaigns that did not survive.

Their volume sat around the 77.3% percentile. The correction has a shape; it should not be applied as a one-size-fits-all rule.

Build record

A real, tested pipeline, not a one-off script.

The build: 21 scripts run against a public Criteo advertising dataset, with seven ordered stages from collecting the data through making the decision.

The checks: separate steps test false alarms, week-to-week stability, and the other questions shown above. Automated tests must pass before a number is trusted.

The record: the dataset, run identifier, and data-shape check are kept with the evidence. The analysis plan was written before the false-alarm results were known; when a final rerun exposed an ordering concern, every later output was regenerated without rewriting the plan.
