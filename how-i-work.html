How I Work

I use AI to move faster without outsourcing the important decisions.

I write down the decision first, let AI speed up the repetitive building, and keep the judgment calls for myself.

Then I map the steps, look for what could break, test the result, and change course when reality disagrees.

See how I check the work ↓ See the projects →
Where I start

Pin down the real question before picking a tool.

A request like “find the best campaigns” or “automate the search” sounds clear until someone has to decide what “best” or “automate” means. I start there.

01 / Decision What decision is this for? A report only helps if a team can do something differently because of it. In the Marketing Measurement study, the decision was which campaigns earned another test.
02 / What could go wrong What could make the answer misleading? Late information, missing records, testing too many things at once, and a convenient definition. In the same study, late credit was the trap.
03 / My call What do I decide myself? Software can collect, compare, and flag. I decide what it can touch, what evidence is enough, and when a result is ready. On the YouTube channel, no clip goes out until I approve it.
One real example

What owning the final explanation looked like when my prediction was wrong.

In the Marketing Measurement study, I wrote down a prediction before the analysis ran: one week of data wouldn’t be reliable enough to agree with the final answer. That was the rule before the result existed.

The result said the opposite, most of the time. I kept my original prediction, labeled it partly wrong, and left it in the record instead of quietly rewriting it. See the result →

Why this matters more than a principle Anyone can say they welcome evidence. The habit is real when the evidence contradicts your own guess and the guess stays visible.
Where AI fits

AI helps me build faster. Deciding what is true stays with me.

I’m open about using AI heavily to build things. That makes checking the work more important. A fast wrong answer is still wrong, and a polished screen can make a weak assumption harder to notice.

A real example: for the Market Scan, AI drafted the 31 collectors and the scoring code. I wrote the checklist, the tests, and the list of posts a filter was allowed to drop, and I read every post the first run threw away.

I own Judgment The question · the limits · the checks · the final explanation
AI assists Repetition Organize · repeat · compare · draft
The final call AI can speed up the work. I still decide what the result means and what happens next.
Lines I keep

Automation should make attention cheap and leave the judgment calls to me.

Every project has a line it won’t cross without a person.

Four areas where automation stops and a person decides.
AreaThe system doesStays with a person

What counts as a matchScores job posts against a checklist.I write and revise the checklist by hand.

How I check the work

Every check here has changed a real answer at least once.

Each one exists because numbers have gone wrong this way before: late data, a moved goalpost, a convenient comparison.

Five recurring checks, the rule behind each, and a real result each one changed.
CheckThe ruleWhat it caught
TimingOnly use what was known when the decision was made.167 of 285 promising campaigns only looked good because of credit that arrived late. See the correction →
Written firstWrite the rule down before seeing the result.2 promising sports ideas were disproved by follow-up checks the rule required. See the sports checks →
Fair comparisonGive every result something fair to beat, like shuffled data.999 shuffles of the marketing data never matched the real result. See the shuffle chart →
Problem logLog every break the day it happens.11 problems logged in the trading lab, typically caught within a day. See the system checks →
A person decidesPassing a check doesn’t approve the next step.97 clips rejected by hand after passing every automatic rule. See the review step →
A rule for any reader If a page gives you a strong conclusion but can’t tell you what was measured, when, and what would change the conclusion, treat it as a pitch.
On a marketing team

The same habits, on a Tuesday.

Before a reportI write down the decision it’s for, so the report answers that question and nothing else.
Before a budget reviewI compare this week’s conversion numbers with a snapshot from a week ago, so late credit can’t make a campaign look better than it did.
After a tracking changeI log it in one line with the date, so a sudden jump in leads can be explained in a minute instead of a meeting.
At the Sales handoffA qualified lead has one written definition that Sales signed off on, and I recheck it every quarter.
