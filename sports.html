Sports research · simulation only

I write down how a sports prediction could be proven wrong before I test it.

Sports are full of hunches, like a team scoring more goals after a long midweek match. Here, every hunch gets a written rule for what would disprove it before any data is checked.

814 questions so far. Only 3 ever looked promising, and all three were later set aside, two of them by my own follow-up checks. By my rough estimate it takes about 15 minutes of my attention a day.

The starting point

"This is what would prove me wrong." Written first, or the test doesn't run.

It's tempting to notice a pattern and then keep adjusting the test until the pattern survives. I flip that: before any test runs, I write down the condition that would end the idea. Researchers call that a falsifier.

Every question also gets a clear definition, a fair comparison, and a time limit. The answer is allowed to be boring.

The automation refreshes the data; I write the rules, read the results, and decide whether an idea moves on.

Every question in the record carries that commitment. The point isn't to look scientific. It's to make the conclusion hard to bend after the fact.
Where ideas stop

Most questions are rejected long before they look promising.

Of 814 logged questions, 3 have ever looked promising enough to test further, and 0 are active in this snapshot. 517 were rejected outright, most because the question was badly set up or the data wasn't good enough.

The integrity loop

An idea can stop at any check, not just the end.

Across 972 total reviews of the data, 43 questions were replaced by a better version, 26 were tested again against fresh data, and 2 were disproved after first looking promising. That last number is the one that matters most: a system that never rejects its own promising ideas isn't really testing them.

Scale and simulated positions

Every prediction is tracked with simulated money only.

The research runs on 2,326,588 data points across 15 ways of framing a question and 6 groups of sports markets, processed in 334 batches. 31 scheduled tasks keep it current; at the snapshot the newest data was 0.5 hours old.

28,007 simulated positions are logged: 20,234 settled and 7,773 still open. Nothing here uses real money. Tracking them shows whether an idea holds up on new data, the way a scientist rechecks a result on a fresh sample.

How all 814 questions reconcile

The first 517 break down into 393 process failures, 118 data failures, 4 grouping problems, and 2 suspicious results. The other 297 reached later checks: 126 failed, 71 did not have enough evidence, 45 failed a fair-comparison check, 43 were replaced by a better version of the question, 9 had no clear answer, and 3 looked promising. Together those groups account for all 814 questions.
