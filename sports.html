Sports research · write the rule first · simulations only

I test sports ideas with a written way to reject them.

In the August 21, 2026 research record, zero ideas cleared the bar for real-money action. That is the system working, not failing.

Before a test runs, I write down what would change my mind. If the check fails, the idea stops.

The starting point

"This is what would prove me wrong." Written first, or it does not run.

The temptation in research is to notice a pattern and then search for the version of the test that lets the pattern survive. I reverse that pressure by writing a condition that ends the idea before the test begins. In research, that stop condition is often called a falsifier.

Before a question enters the workflow, it gets a definition, a fair comparison, a time boundary, and a condition that would end the idea. The result is allowed to be boring.

The automation refreshes the data; I write the rule, inspect the result, and decide whether the idea advances.

Every question in the evidence record carries a commitment. The point is not to make the research look more scientific. It is to make the conclusion harder to move after the fact.

Illustrative only, not a hidden real case: this is what a written rejection rule looks like before it enters the system.

01 The idea. After a team plays a long midweek match, its next weekend game produces more goals than the usual baseline.
02 What would prove me wrong. If the pattern disappears after accounting for opponent strength and home advantage, or fails in a season kept out of the first test, the idea is dead.
03 The time limit. 500 observations, or the end of the season, whichever comes first.
04 What happened. If it fails the pre-written condition, it is rejected and does not get a second look. The rule exists before there is any reason to want the idea to survive.
Where ideas actually die

Most questions are rejected before they get anywhere close to a bet.

Of 814 logged questions, 3 have ever reached candidate status and 0 are active in this snapshot. 517 were rejected outright, most on process or data grounds, well before the question ever got a real look.

See how all 814 questions reconcile

The first 517 break down into 393 process failures, 118 data failures, 4 grouping problems, and 2 suspicious results. The other 297 reached later checks: 126 failed, 71 did not have enough evidence, 45 failed a fair-comparison check, 43 were replaced by a better version of the question, 9 had no clear answer, and 3 reached candidate status. Together those groups account for all 814 questions.

The integrity loop

An idea can stop at any check, not just the end.

Across 972 total reviews of the data, 43 questions were replaced by a better version, 26 were tested again against fresh data, and 2 were disproved after first looking promising. That last number is the one that matters most: a system that never rejects its own early candidates is not really testing them.

The scale underneath

2,326,588 observations, 334 batches.

The research covers 15 ways of framing a question and 6 broad groups of sports markets. 31 scheduled tasks keep it current, and the underlying data is refreshed every 0.5 hours.

Simulated bets

The record tracks simulated bets, not real stakes.

28,007 simulated positions are logged: 20,234 settled and 7,773 still open. Nothing here uses real money. The record asks whether an idea survives new market data before it could ever justify risking money.

What it took

The interesting part is how often the answer was no.

Time and scale
814 questions moved through a dated record with written rejection rules, comparisons, and follow-up checks.
What broke
Early ideas failed process, data, comparison, or fresh-data checks; two that looked promising were later disproved.
Next time
I would make the rejection reason visible at the first gate, so a reader never has to infer why a question stopped.
Why it belongs
The system makes “not enough evidence” a recorded result, not a silent absence of a winner.
