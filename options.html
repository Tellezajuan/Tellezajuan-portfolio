Options research · simulations only · no real money

I test options ideas before they reach real money.

Every idea is written down, tested, checked, and kept on paper until the evidence is strong enough.

I built a simulation-only system to challenge promising ideas. It records each question, replays it against historical data, and tries to break the result with fair comparisons before anything could reach real money.

The ordinary-language version

An idea has to survive the whole loop.

01Write the question. Say what the idea is supposed to explain.
02Decide what would prove it wrong. Set the comparison and failure rule before running the test.
03Run the historical check. Replay the question against past data and keep the underlying records.
04Challenge the result. Compare it with a simpler explanation and keep the trade on paper.
05Record what happened. Pass, fail, noise, or not enough data all stay in the dated record.
06Reconcile before the next run. Check what changed and keep real-money decisions out of the loop.
The problem

Finding something interesting is easy. Knowing whether it is real is the job.

A historical test can produce a beautiful line and still answer a question nobody wrote down in advance. The danger is not that the result is ugly. It is that the result becomes persuasive before anyone asks what produced it by accident.

This project makes the challenge part of the system: finite questions, explicit controls, paper execution, and a record of what got rejected. Markets are the harshest place to learn that a persuasive chart can be nothing. That is the same lesson a campaign dashboard teaches, only faster and with a scoreboard.

The finding

Most ideas do not survive a fair comparison.

The scale of the check

The conclusions rest on a much larger evidence set.

15,412 historical-test results and 988,538 individual simulated-trade records fed into those 446 conclusions, over 6,479.2 hours of computer time. The conclusions are the short answer; these are the records underneath them.

System checks

The research is only as trustworthy as the system running it.

A conclusion is only as good as the steps that produced it, so those steps are checked on a normal cadence—not just when a result looks surprising.

TESTS 952 automated tests passing; 48.8% of code lines exercised. That is not full coverage. The number is a minimum safety check, not a finish line.
INCIDENTS 11 problems logged; the middle one was noticed in 24.0 hours. Each incident stays in the internal log with what broke and when the system caught it. This portfolio shows the count and one sanitized example because hidden failures do not make a research engine more trustworthy.
ONE INCIDENT, SANITIZED A broken run stayed out of the result record. A scheduled run produced incomplete evidence, so the health check stopped it before a conclusion could be recorded. After the missing coverage was repaired, the run was repeated and the incident remained in the log.
AUTOMATION 34 scheduled tasks keep the system moving. The research can run on a normal cadence without the owner watching every stage constantly. That changes the workload, not the decision boundary.
What it took

A passing result is still not proof.

Time and scale
15,412 historical-test results, 988,538 individual simulated-trade records, and a recurring operating cadence made the evidence expensive enough to respect.
What broke
One incomplete run was stopped before it could enter the result record; the problem stayed visible after repair.
Next time
I would surface coverage gaps even earlier and keep the “not enough evidence” path as prominent as the pass path.
Why it belongs
The system makes a conservative decision—stay on paper—while showing exactly how the checks and failures shaped it.
